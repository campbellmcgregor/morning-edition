Mobile Security
Directly applies to you

Android May Soon Restrict On-Device ADB

A quiet change buried in Android's developer options could close one of the most abused social-engineering pathways on the platform: the on-device debugging bridge that scammers routinely talk victims into enabling to seize control of a phone.

Android Debug Bridge has always been a power tool — the same interface developers use to sideload builds and read logs also grants near-total control over a device once activated. For years, fraud rings have exploited that duality, coaching victims over the phone to switch on wireless debugging and hand attackers a remote foothold without a single malicious app being installed. The reported change would gate on-device ADB behind stronger friction, narrowing a gap that no antivirus signature ever caught because nothing about it was, technically, malware.

For anyone running a mobile fleet, this is a reminder that the softest attacks bypass software controls entirely. MDM policies can disable developer options on managed devices, but the sprawling BYOD estate most organisations actually carry is where the risk lives. Google tightening the default is welcome, yet it is still "may soon" — a signal, not a shipped mitigation. The lesson for defenders is that user-walkthrough fraud has matured to the point where platform vendors are re-architecting around it, and awareness training should now name ADB and "developer mode" explicitly.

What to do

Audit your MDM baseline now: confirm developer options and USB/wireless debugging are disabled on all managed Android devices, and add a specific "never enable developer mode when a caller asks you to" line to security-awareness material. Treat this as a coming default, not a mitigation you can yet rely on.

Surveillance & Privacy

The Vigilante Movement Knocking Out Flock's Cameras

Across American towns, a loose network of residents has begun physically disabling the automatic licence-plate readers that Flock Safety has quietly bolted to street corners — a grassroots revolt against a surveillance grid few communities ever voted for.

Flock's cameras log the movements of every passing vehicle, building a searchable history of where people go that police departments can query — and, as reporting has repeatedly shown, that data gets shared far beyond the local force. The Guardian's account traces the counter-movement: people cutting cables, bagging lenses, and mapping camera locations, framing it as civil disobedience against warrantless mass tracking. Whatever one makes of the tactics, the story marks a shift from abstract privacy debate to physical confrontation with the hardware itself.

The security-leadership read here is about the normalisation of pervasive sensor networks and the backlash they eventually provoke. Automatic plate readers are a data-broker business wrapped in a public-safety pitch, and the same pattern — cheap sensors, centralised retention, opaque sharing agreements — is spreading into corporate physical security. Anyone deploying camera analytics on their own estate should study how quickly community consent evaporates when retention and sharing are left undefined, because the reputational blast radius is not theoretical.

AI & Data Control

Cloudflare Hands Customers a Switch for AI Crawlers

Cloudflare is giving the sites behind its network granular control over how AI companies scrape their content — block it, meter it, or charge for it — turning what was an invisible extraction into a policy decision that site owners actually get to make.

The announcement extends Cloudflare's "content independence" push: operators can now distinguish training crawlers from search and answer-engine bots and apply different rules to each, including pay-per-crawl arrangements. For years the default was that anything public was fair game for model training, with no consent step and no compensation. Putting the toggle in the customer's hands reframes AI scraping as a negotiable relationship rather than a silent tax on everyone who publishes on the open web.

For a security and data-governance owner, this is a control worth wiring into policy, not just skimming as marketing. If your organisation publishes anything — documentation, research, customer-facing content — you now have a lever over whether it feeds third-party models, which matters for both IP posture and data-leakage risk. The flip side is dependency: relying on a single intermediary to arbitrate AI access concentrates real power in Cloudflare's hands. Useful today, but note the leverage you are handing over.

Edge AI

A 28.9M-Parameter LLM Running on an $8 Microcontroller

A developer has squeezed a small language model onto an ESP32-class chip costing a few dollars, running inference entirely on-device with no cloud round-trip — a proof that useful language models are escaping the datacentre.

The project fits a 28.9-million-parameter model into the tight memory of a cheap microcontroller, trading raw capability for total locality. It will not draft your email, but it demonstrates that constrained, single-purpose language models can live at the very edge — in sensors, appliances, and toys — where connectivity is optional and latency is measured in the silicon, not the network. The engineering is as much about quantisation and careful memory layout as it is about the model itself.

The security implication of on-device inference cuts both ways. On the upside, data that never leaves the chip cannot be intercepted in transit or harvested by a cloud provider — a genuine privacy win for edge deployments. On the downside, a fleet of tiny models embedded in physical devices becomes a new, hard-to-patch attack surface, and "the AI runs locally" is already becoming a marketing claim that obscures firmware you cannot inspect. Watch this space: edge LLMs will land in procurement long before governance catches up.

Field Notes on Agentic Coding, Benchmarks, and What Actually Works

Dan Luu turns his characteristically sceptical eye on agentic coding — the LLM benchmarks that mislead, the test processes that hold up, and the gap between demo-day claims and what survives contact with a real codebase.

Luu's notes push against the hype cycle without dismissing the tools. His argument is that headline benchmarks tell you little about production usefulness, and that the teams extracting real value from agentic coding are the ones who invest in test harnesses, verification, and process discipline rather than trusting model output at face value. It is a practitioner's account: less about which model tops a leaderboard and more about the scaffolding that makes any of them safe to lean on.

For anyone building AI-assisted workflows — exactly the ground you work on with Claude Code — this is the counterweight to vendor decks. The takeaway is that the agent is only as trustworthy as the tests around it, and that "the model did it" is not a control. Treat generated code as untrusted input, gate it behind the same review and CI you would apply to a junior engineer, and measure outcomes on your own repositories rather than on synthetic benchmarks. The discipline is the product.

What to do

Before scaling any agentic coding pattern, define the verification loop first: which tests must pass, what a human reviews, and what metric proves it is actually working on your codebase. Adopt Luu's framing — benchmarks are marketing; your own regression suite is the ground truth.

The New Rules of Context Engineering for Claude 5

Anthropic has published a reworked playbook for feeding context to its Claude 5-generation models, shifting the craft away from prompt tricks and toward the deliberate management of what the model sees, remembers, and retrieves.

The guidance reflects how much has changed as context windows have grown and models have become better at using long inputs: the bottleneck is no longer fitting information in but curating it well. The new rules emphasise structuring context, managing memory across a session, and being intentional about what you retrieve rather than dumping everything and hoping. For heavy users of Claude, it is a direct update to daily practice — the difference between a model that flails and one that performs is increasingly in the context engineering, not the prompt wording.

This sits squarely in the toolkit you build on. Anyone running Claude Code or Cowork workflows should read the source rather than a summary, because the specifics — how to lay out context, when to summarise, how memory is handled — translate straight into better, cheaper, more reliable runs. It also signals where Anthropic thinks the skill ceiling is moving: away from clever one-shot prompts and toward engineering the whole information environment the model operates in.

What to do

Read the primary guidance and revisit your Claude Code and Cowork setups against it — particularly how you structure long context, manage session memory, and control retrieval. Small changes here compound across every automated workflow you run.

DeepSeek Pauses Fundraising After Leaked Compute-Gap Remarks

A leaked transcript of DeepSeek founder Liang Wenfeng candidly conceding how far Chinese labs trail the US on compute has reportedly forced the company to pause a fundraise — an unusually raw admission escaping into public view.

The document, circulating as a translated PDF, captures investor-meeting remarks in which the compute gap between DeepSeek and its American rivals is discussed with more honesty than the usual public posture. Whether the pause stems from the leak's substance or simply the embarrassment of private candour becoming public, the episode is a reminder that the most damaging disclosures are often not stolen credentials but stolen candour — words meant for a closed room.

The information-security angle is the leak itself. Investor meetings, board decks, and executive calls are among the highest-value targets precisely because they contain unguarded strategic truth, and they are frequently the least protected. The controls that matter — who can record, how transcripts are stored, what is permitted to leave the room — are governance questions, not firewall questions. DeepSeek's discomfort is a case study in why executive communications deserve the same data-classification rigour as any customer database.

Open-Weight AI

Open-Weight AI Is Having Its Kubernetes Moment

The argument that open-weight models are following the same trajectory Kubernetes took — from scrappy alternative to default infrastructure — is gaining traction, and it reframes the closed-versus-open debate as a question of where the ecosystem's gravity finally settles.

The comparison is deliberate: Kubernetes won not because it was the best single product but because an open standard attracted an ecosystem that proprietary offerings could not match. The thesis holds that open-weight models are on the same path, with tooling, fine-tuning, and deployment coalescing around freely available weights. If it plays out, the strategic centre of gravity in AI shifts from a handful of API providers toward whoever builds the best infrastructure around open models.

For a security leader, the appeal of open weights is control: models you can run in your own environment, inspect, and keep off third-party servers. The cost is that you inherit the full supply-chain and patching burden — provenance of weights, poisoned fine-tunes, and the operational weight of running inference yourself. The Kubernetes analogy cuts here too: powerful, open, and notoriously easy to misconfigure straight into a breach. Plan for the governance, not just the download.

AI Governance

Debian Puts LLM Usage to a Vote

Debian, one of the oldest and most consensus-driven open-source projects, is formally voting on how — and whether — large language models may be used in its work, a governance experiment the rest of the software world should watch closely.

The ballot puts three proposals to the project's developers, ranging in how permissively they treat AI-generated contributions. Debian doing this in the open, through its established voting process, is itself the story: rather than letting policy emerge ad hoc from individual maintainers, the project is trying to set a collective, documented position on provenance, licensing, and acceptable use. It is slow, procedural, and exactly the kind of deliberate governance most organisations skip.

Every organisation now faces the same question Debian is voting on — what are the rules for AI-generated code and content — and most have answered it by default, which is to say not at all. Debian's proposals are a useful template precisely because they force the trade-offs into the open: licensing exposure, contribution provenance, and the risk of ingesting output whose training data you cannot vouch for. Borrow the discipline even if you do not borrow the specific rules: write the policy down before practice sets it for you.

Censorship Resistance

Bitchat Finds a Home on Radicle

After a government order pushed the Bluetooth mesh-messaging app Bitchat off GitHub, its code has resurfaced on Radicle, a peer-to-peer, sovereign-less code-hosting network — a quiet demonstration that removal from one platform is no longer removal from the internet.

Bitchat, associated with Jack Dorsey, drew official pressure over its offline, infrastructure-independent messaging. Its move to Radicle — where repositories live across a distributed network rather than on a single company's servers — closes a loop that has been forming for years: as takedowns become a routine instrument of control, the tools being suppressed migrate to substrates that have no takedown button to press. The code is not hidden; it is simply hosted somewhere no single authority can reach.

The reflective point is about the shifting terrain under content control. Centralised platforms gave governments and companies a single lever; decentralised hosting removes it, for better and worse. The same properties that protect a censored messaging app also shelter genuinely harmful projects, and there is no version of this technology that serves only the sympathetic case. For anyone who thinks about trust, resilience, and control, Radicle is worth understanding not as a novelty but as a preview of where the fight over who can erase what is heading.
