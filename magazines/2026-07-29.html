Codex Security

OpenAI has shipped a command-line scanner and TypeScript SDK that hunts vulnerabilities across a codebase, tracks findings over time and drops into CI — and it will happily authenticate with a personal ChatGPT sign-in to do it.

The tool itself is unremarkable in shape: scan the repo, keep a history in a local workbench state directory, wire it into the pipeline, run interactive or automated. Node 22 and Python 3.10 are prerequisites. What is worth sitting with is the position it stakes out. The model vendor has moved into vulnerability management, and the price of entry is shipping your source tree through their inference. That is a defensible trade — LLM triage genuinely does better than pattern matching on the class of bug that spans three files — but it is a trade, and it is being made at the developer's desk rather than in a procurement review.

The detail that should get your attention is the authentication precedence. Supporting both ChatGPT sign-in and API keys, with flags to control which wins, means an engineer can point this at a private repository using their own consumer account, entirely outside your identity provider, your logging and your data processing agreement. That is the shadow-AI problem in a new wrapper, and this wrapper has 2.7k stars and a three-command quickstart. The scanner will also be right often enough to be defended internally, which makes it harder to remove later than it is to govern now.

What to do

Decide this before someone installs it. Check whether a developer on your estate can npm-install a scanner that authenticates with a personal ChatGPT account and point it at private code — if your egress rules and package policy do not already answer that, this is the week to make them answer. If you do adopt it, mandate API-key auth bound to an organisation credential rather than individual sign-in, and confirm the workbench state directory is gitignored before findings history ends up in a repository.

Stop Killing the Internet: No Digital ID and No Age Verification

A registered European Citizens' Initiative is collecting signatures against mandatory digital identity and age verification, on the argument that a web where every reader must first prove who they are is not the web anyone agreed to.

The mechanism is worth understanding before the politics. An ECI that gathers a million signatures across at least seven member states within twelve months compels the Commission to respond formally — it does not compel it to legislate, and most initiatives never clear the threshold. So the direct legal effect here is likely to be nil. The signal is not nil. Age assurance duties are already live in the UK under the Online Safety Act, the EU has been piloting age verification tooling, and the identity wallet under eIDAS 2 is arriving regardless. An organised push-back at this stage tells you the political consensus is thinner than the compliance calendar suggests.

The security argument deserves more airtime than the civil liberties one, because it is the part that lands on your desk. Universal age verification creates a new identity honeypot at every content site that has to comply — and the operators of those sites are publishers and platforms, not banks. They will hold document scans or biometric estimates with the operational maturity of a company whose core competence is something else entirely. Every one of those becomes a breach with a category of data that cannot be rotated. Concentrating that into a handful of accredited verifiers reduces the count of targets and raises the value of each.

What to do

If any product surface you own is in scope for UK Online Safety Act age assurance, or is preparing for an EU equivalent, make the data-protection design decision now rather than at launch. Prefer a third-party attribute assertion — an over-18 yes or no — over collecting documents yourself; set retention in seconds rather than months; and write down, before you need it, who owns the incident if the verifier you chose is the one that gets breached.

DMARC has been public since 2012 but most company domains still don't enforce it

CipherCue measured 67,336 domains between April and July and found 68.4% either publish no DMARC record at all or publish one set to p=none — fourteen years after the standard was finished, two thirds of the sample still cannot refuse a forgery.

The distribution is the story: 45.1% with no record, 23.3% at p=none, 15.2% at quarantine, 16.3% at reject. Nobody is stuck at the beginning — they are stuck one step in. Publishing p=none takes an afternoon and produces a green tick on a questionnaire, which is precisely why so many organisations stop there. Moving to quarantine or reject requires knowing, with confidence, every legitimate system that sends mail as you: the payroll provider, the ticketing system, the marketing platform someone in a regional office signed up for in 2019. That inventory is the actual work, and it has never had a budget line.

The genuinely new finding is the reporting bottleneck. Across 36,974 records the study counted 10,268 distinct rua reporting domains, and 79% of them appeared exactly once — frequently as hashed mailboxes rather than a recognisable vendor. The aggregate reports that exist specifically to tell you who sends on your behalf arrive as an unattributable list, converting a configuration change into an open-ended research task. Worth naming the obvious: CipherCue sells a product built to solve this exact problem, so the conclusion is convenient. The dataset is still the dataset, and the mechanism it describes matches what anyone who has run a DMARC rollout will recognise.

What to do

Pull your own DMARC record today and read the policy. If it says p=none, you have a monitoring posture wearing a security label. Give the move to reject a named owner and a date, budget the sender-discovery work honestly as weeks rather than an afternoon, and — this is the part that gets missed — check your subsidiary, acquisition and parked domains. Those are what actually get spoofed, and they are disproportionately the ones with no record at all.

Protocol Release
Directly applies to you

MCP 2026-07-28 Specification: transport going stateless

The initialize handshake is gone and so is the Mcp-Session-Id header. Every request now carries its own protocol version, client identity and capabilities, so calls can land on any server instance behind an ordinary load balancer with no shared storage.

Two changes matter operationally. Method and tool names now travel in Mcp-Method and Mcp-Name HTTP headers, which means gateways, rate limiters and firewalls can route and authorise on headers instead of parsing JSON bodies — policy enforcement moves to the edge, where the existing controls already live. That is a real security improvement, not a convenience. Server-initiated requests over held-open streams are replaced by Multi Round-Trip Requests: a server returns resultType input_required with the inputs it needs and the client retries with answers attached. List results for tools, prompts and resources gain ttlMs and cacheScope hints.

Read the authorisation section twice. RFC 9207 issuer validation is now required — clients must validate the iss parameter before redeeming an authorisation code. Client credentials are bound to the authorisation server that issued them, closing cross-server reuse. Dynamic Client Registration is deprecated in favour of Client ID Metadata Documents. On the deprecation clock: Roots, Sampling and Logging carry a twelve-month minimum support window, and the legacy HTTP+SSE transport gets a one-year offramp. Anything that relied on session state must now mint explicit handles from tools instead.

What to do

If you run MCP servers — and with Claude Code in the workflow you almost certainly do — inventory which ones hold session state, because those are the ones that break. Read the auth changes before the transport changes: credential binding and the move from DCR to CIMD affect anything stood up with dynamic registration. All four Tier 1 SDKs shipped with migration guidance. The offramps are generous, but they are clocks, and nothing schedules itself.

Discovering Cryptographic Weaknesses with Claude

Anthropic's Frontier Red Team used a preview model to find a nontrivial automorphism in the lattice underlying HAWK — halving the effective strength of a NIST post-quantum signature candidate — and to sharpen a meet-in-the-middle attack against seven-round AES-128.

These are flaws in the mathematics, not in someone's implementation, which is the unusual part. The HAWK result exploits previously unknown symmetries in the lattice and drops HAWK-256 from roughly 2^64 to 2^38. It took about sixty hours across a week, running semi-autonomously in an agentic harness with occasional human steering, at somewhere near $100,000 in API spend. The AES work ran almost entirely unattended — three minimal prompts over three days and around a billion output tokens — producing a 200 to 800 times speedup via a novel fingerprinting step that eliminates one enumeration guess. Lesser results landed on LEA, Serpent-128, Salsa20, Poseidon and SHA-1.

Anthropic states the caveats plainly and they matter. Neither attack touches anything you are running. HAWK is a candidate under review, not a deployed scheme; the AES result applies to a reduced-round variant under a chosen-plaintext model requiring on the order of 2^105 encryptions. Human researchers spent considerably longer validating the findings than the model spent producing them. The thing to take away is not that a cipher fell — it is the cost curve. A hundred thousand dollars and a week of wall-clock now buys a genuine cryptanalytic advance against a standardisation candidate, and that number has only ever gone one direction.

What to do

Nothing to patch. What should shift is a planning assumption: if your post-quantum migration treats NIST candidate selection as a settled endpoint, note that the same class of tooling now reviewing those candidates is available to everyone else on the same budget. Keep crypto-agility — the ability to swap a primitive without re-architecting the system around it — on the engineering roadmap as a requirement with an owner, not as a line on a strategy slide.

Kimi K3 Architecture Overview and Notes

Sebastian Raschka walks Moonshot's latest open-weights release choice by choice — an annotated teardown for people who want to know what actually changed rather than which column of the benchmark table went up.

Raschka's architecture write-ups have become the reliable genre in a field that mostly produces launch copy. The value is not novelty; it is that someone reads the config, the paper and the code together and explains the design decisions in the order an engineer would need them. Yesterday this edition carried the weights release itself. Today it carries the explanation, which is the more durable artefact — the weights will be superseded within a quarter, the architectural reasoning will still be legible a year out.

Why a security leader should care about a model teardown at all: open-weights models are the ones that end up running inside your perimeter, on someone's workstation or a rented GPU, precisely because your policy says not to send the data to an API. Understanding the architecture is understanding the deployment footprint — memory profile, context handling, serving cost, what it takes to run it badly. When the request lands on your desk to allow local inference, the useful question is not whether the model is good but what shape of infrastructure it drags in behind it.

Supply Chain & OT

FCC bans foreign-produced solar inverters, grid lockout begins today

pv magazine reports that the FCC has barred foreign-produced solar inverters from grid connection in the US, with the lockout effective immediately — country of origin has become a compliance property of a piece of power electronics.

The reporting is a day old and the detail is still settling, so treat the specifics as provisional. The mechanism, though, is entirely legible. Inverters are internet-connected, remotely updatable devices sitting between distributed generation and the grid, deployed at enormous scale, with firmware controlled by whoever manufactured them. That is the same risk shape regulators identified in telecoms equipment a decade ago, and they are reaching for the same instrument: exclude the vendor rather than attempt to verify the device. It is a blunt tool used because the precise one — provable firmware provenance at scale — does not exist.

For a European organisation the ruling does not apply, but the precedent travels. This is now the third or fourth asset class where provenance has moved from a procurement preference to a regulatory attribute, and the direction is one-way. If you own operational technology, the question worth asking this week is whether you can answer "who can push firmware to this device" for every internet-connected thing in the estate. Most organisations cannot. Note the trade-off honestly, too: rip-and-replace mandates manufacture their own risk, because an installed base that becomes non-compliant overnight is an installed base that stops receiving updates.

Model Economics

A $500 RL fine-tune of a 9B open model beat frontier models on catalog review

A narrow, well-specified task, a small open-weights model, a few hundred dollars of reinforcement learning — and, the authors report, a result that outperforms the frontier labs on the single job it was trained to do.

The claim is narrow, which is exactly what makes it plausible. Catalog review is a bounded, repetitive judgement task with abundant labelled history sitting in a database somewhere — the regime where task-specific fine-tuning has always beaten general capability. The finding is not that small models are secretly better. It is that the price of finding out has collapsed. Five hundred dollars is a corporate card and an afternoon, not a business case, an approval and a quarter. That is a change in kind rather than degree, because it removes the friction that used to make these projects visible.

Which is where it becomes a governance story. A frontier API arrives with a vendor, a data processing agreement, a status page, an abuse team and someone to call. A nine-billion-parameter model that an engineer tuned on a rented GPU has none of those, and it will be holding your catalog — which in most organisations means supplier data, pricing and occasionally things nobody realised were in there. Expect these to appear without a procurement conversation, because the entire premise is that they are too cheap to need one. The control that works is not a ban; it is making the cheap path pass through a place you can see.

Security Architecture

Google's Beyond Zero: Enterprise Security for the AI Era

Google is back in ACM Queue with an argument about what enterprise security has to become once AI agents are first-class actors inside the estate — the same venue, and the same house style, that produced BeyondCorp.

The lineage is the reason to pay attention. BeyondCorp arrived as a description of how Google secured itself internally and became, within a few years, the reference text that an entire zero-trust product category was sold against. When this organisation writes down its own architecture in this particular journal, the vendor landscape tends to rearrange itself around the vocabulary about three years later. Worth knowing before you click: ACM's site is returning 403 to a good number of readers today, so the Hacker News thread is currently doing much of the work of distribution and summary.

The question it raises is worth holding regardless of where the article lands on it. Zero trust was built to authenticate a user, a device, and eventually a workload. An agent is none of those cleanly. It acts on a human's behalf but holds its own credentials; it operates at machine speed across systems that assumed a human pace; and its authorisation properly belongs to an intent — "reconcile this invoice" — rather than to a session that stays open until something revokes it. Identity teams do not currently have a primitive for that. If you are being asked to approve agentic tooling this quarter, that missing primitive is the gap you are papering over with policy.

AI Epistemics

Truth is not a direction: a Tarski attack on LLM probes

A formal argument that the linear probes used to read truthfulness out of a language model's activations are hunting for something that, by Tarski's undefinability theorem, cannot sit along a single direction in the first place.

Interpretability's most practically useful result to date has been that you can often find a direction in activation space correlating with some property — sentiment, refusal, deception, truth — and then read it, or steer along it. The truth probes are the ones people reach for when arguing that we will eventually be able to tell when a model is lying to us. Jansma's argument runs against that: truth is self-referential in a way a linear functional cannot capture, and Tarski established that no sufficiently expressive language can define its own truth predicate. The probe finds something, but the something is not what the label claims.

Hold it lightly. An impossibility result about a clean formalisation does not automatically kill the messy empirical thing, and probes that correlate usefully with honesty remain useful even if they are not measuring truth as such. But it is a needed corrective. A great many AI assurance roadmaps quietly assume that "we will detect deception through interpretability" is an engineering problem with a delivery date attached. If the honest position is that we can build serviceable heuristics but not a truth detector, that materially changes what anyone should be promising a board about model oversight — and it is better to know now than during the incident.
