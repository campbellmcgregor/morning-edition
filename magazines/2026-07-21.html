Critical Incident
Directly applies to you

Hacker wipes Romania's land registry database

An intruder did not encrypt or ransom Romania's national cadastre — they deleted it, taking the authoritative record of who owns what across an entire country offline and turning a security incident into a sovereignty problem.

Destruction is the failure mode almost nobody rehearses. Ransomware at least leaves the data in place and gives you a counterparty with an incentive to hand back a key; a wiper leaves nothing to negotiate with. Every tabletop exercise that ends in “and then we pay or we restore” quietly assumes the second option exists. A land registry is the sharpest possible illustration of why that assumption deserves testing: property ownership is not a dataset that can be reconstructed from downstream copies or inferred from logs. If the registry is authoritative and the registry is gone, the legal fact it recorded is gone with it until a backup proves otherwise.

The detail worth watching as this unfolds is not how the attacker got in — that will turn out to be credentials, an exposed admin interface, or an unpatched edge device, as it nearly always does. It is whether the backups survived. Wipers routinely go for backup infrastructure first, because attackers understand recovery capability is the real target. Online snapshots on the same domain, replicas that faithfully replicate a DELETE, and restore procedures nobody has actually executed at full scale are the three ways organisations discover on the day that they had backup software rather than a recovery capability. Romania will now find out which it had, in public.

What to do

Pick your single most authoritative dataset — the one no other system can reconstruct — and answer three questions in writing this week: is at least one copy immutable or genuinely offline, is it on a separate identity plane so domain admin cannot reach it, and when did someone last perform a full restore rather than a file-level spot check? If the last answer is “never” or “over a year ago”, schedule the restore test and treat destruction — not encryption — as the scenario in your next tabletop.

Surveillance & Privacy

Flock credibility lost as it repeatedly lies to city councils, police, and public

The ACLU documents a pattern in which the automated licence plate reader vendor told councils its cameras did one thing while the systems and contracts did another, across multiple jurisdictions and multiple years.

The specific claims matter less than the structural lesson. Surveillance procurement runs on vendor assertion: a council asks whether data is shared with federal agencies, whether it is retained beyond thirty days, whether searches are audited, and the vendor answers. There is rarely a technical control, a contractual penalty, or an independent audit standing behind the answer. When the answer turns out to be wrong, the discovery happens years later through public records requests rather than through any mechanism the buyer put in place. That is a procurement failure as much as a vendor one, and it repeats wherever the buyer lacks the expertise to verify what they are told.

For anyone running third-party risk, this is the ALPR-shaped version of a problem that shows up in every SaaS review. The questionnaire response is not the control. If a vendor tells you data stays in-region, that claim needs to be a contractual commitment with an audit right and, ideally, a technical constraint you can observe — not a tick in a spreadsheet that ages quietly for three years. The Flock story is unusually well documented, which makes it a useful artefact to hand to a procurement team that thinks a completed security questionnaire is the end of the conversation rather than the start of it.

Threat Landscape
Directly applies to you

Reverse-engineering is cheap now

Simon Willison argues that language models have collapsed the cost of understanding compiled binaries, minified bundles, and undocumented protocols from expert-weeks to commodity-hours — which quietly retires a whole category of security assumption.

A great deal of shipped software is protected by nothing more than the tedium of taking it apart. Obfuscated JavaScript, an undocumented mobile API, a licence check buried in a stripped binary, a proprietary wire format — none of these are cryptographic protections, they are economic ones. They work because the attacker's time was worth more than the prize. When a model can chew through a decompiled function and explain its logic in a minute, that economic moat drains. The protection was never the obscurity; it was the labour cost of penetrating it, and that cost has just fallen through the floor.

The practical consequence is a shift in who can find your bugs. Vulnerability research on closed-source products used to be a specialist trade with a small talent pool. Lowering the skill floor widens the pool on both sides — defenders get cheaper analysis of the binaries in their estate, and so does everyone else. Expect faster turnaround from patch to weaponised exploit as diffing becomes conversational, and expect hardcoded secrets in client-side code to be found at scale rather than by chance. Anything you shipped on the assumption that nobody would bother looking should now be treated as read.

What to do

Inventory what your organisation ships to endpoints — mobile apps, desktop agents, browser bundles, firmware — and ask what each one would reveal if fully understood. Prioritise hunting for embedded credentials, API keys, and client-side authorisation logic, since those are the findings this capability shift surfaces first. Then compress your patch window: if exploit development is getting faster, your mean time to deploy a vendor fix is now the variable that matters most.

04
Authentication

Opaque, interoperable passkey records (and a Go API)

Filippo Valsorda proposes a design in which the credential a relying party stores for a passkey is a single opaque blob, letting the WebAuthn library evolve its internals without every application rewriting its database schema.

The unglamorous reason passkey rollouts stall is not user experience — it is that WebAuthn asks application developers to persist a scatter of protocol-specific fields they do not understand: public key, credential ID, signature counter, transports, attestation format, backup eligibility flags. Each field carries subtle security semantics, each new spec revision adds more, and the storage layer ends up frozen around whatever the library needed on the day it was integrated. Making the record opaque moves that complexity behind an interface where it belongs, and means a library upgrade can change what is stored without a migration.

This is a good example of a security improvement that arrives as an ergonomics change. The counter-tracking and backup-state semantics that most integrations get wrong are exactly the things a library can handle correctly once it owns the format outright. If your organisation is somewhere in the long march from passwords and SMS codes to phishing-resistant authentication, the engineering friction in your own applications is a bigger obstacle than anything on the standards side, and designs like this are what erode it. Worth reading if you are the person being asked why the passkey project keeps slipping a quarter.

AI & Research
Directly applies to you

Claude Fable produced a counterexample to the Jacobian Conjecture

A model appears to have generated a counterexample to a problem that has stood open since 1939, and the mathematical community is now doing the slow, necessary work of checking whether it holds — a genre of story that keeps recurring this month.

Two things are true at once and both matter. First, extraordinary claims in mathematics get verified or demolished within days, and the honest position this morning is that verification is in progress. Second, even a counterexample that eventually fails scrutiny signals something real about where these systems have got to: producing a candidate object that specialists consider worth checking against an eighty-year-old conjecture is a materially different capability from producing a plausible-looking proof sketch. A companion front-page post today notes that human mathematicians are increasingly being “outcounterexampled” — a much better description of the current frontier than most benchmark scores.

Counterexample generation is search over a space where the answer is cheap to check and expensive to find. That asymmetry is exactly the shape of a great deal of security work: finding an input that breaks a parser, a sequence of permissions that composes into privilege escalation, a configuration that satisfies every policy while producing an unintended path. Nobody should extrapolate from a Jacobian counterexample to automated exploit discovery in a straight line. But it is a reasonable prompt to ask which of your assurance problems are actually search problems with cheap verification, because those are the ones where this trajectory lands first.

What to do

Nothing to patch here — read it as a capability marker rather than a task. When you next set expectations with your board or your engineering leads about what AI assistance can and cannot do, note that the frontier is moving fastest on problems where candidate answers are hard to generate but cheap to verify, and slowest where verification itself requires judgement. That distinction will age better than any current benchmark.

AI Tooling

Kimi Work

Moonshot ships an agentic work product on top of its Kimi models, pushing the Chinese labs' competition with Western vendors out of the benchmark table and into the applications layer where enterprises actually buy.

The interesting move is the packaging. For two years the Chinese frontier labs competed on open weights and price, which mattered enormously to developers and almost not at all to the average enterprise buyer, who does not want a model — they want something that reads their documents, drafts their deliverables, and connects to their systems. A product like this closes that gap, and it does so at a moment when the open-weights story has become a genuine strategic advantage rather than a consolation prize. The competitive pressure on Western application vendors is about to get considerably more direct.

The security questions arrive with it. An agentic work tool wants broad read access to documents, mail, and internal systems, which makes the data residency and processing questions immediate rather than theoretical for any UK or EU organisation weighing it up. Where does inference happen, what is retained, what does the sub-processor list look like, and can any of it be constrained contractually? Those are answerable questions and worth asking early, because the pattern from every previous productivity wave is that individual teams adopt first and governance arrives afterwards. The interesting version of this story is not whether it is good — it is whether it turns up in your estate before you have a position on it.

Open Source Governance

Jellyfin founder leaves the project team

The founder of the widely deployed open-source media server has stepped away from the project, prompting the familiar and uncomfortable question of what happens to governance, release signing, and security response when a keystone contributor departs.

Every organisation runs open-source software whose bus factor it has never examined. The dependency scanner reports versions and known vulnerabilities; it says nothing about whether the project still has maintainers willing to triage a security report on a Sunday, who holds the signing keys, or whether the release pipeline depends on one person's personal infrastructure. Founder departures are the moment those questions become answerable, because the project has to redistribute responsibilities in public. Jellyfin is a well-run project with a real community, so the likely outcome here is an orderly transition — but the general case is what should concern anyone maintaining a software bill of materials.

The supply chain risk is not that a maintainer leaves. It is the interval afterwards, when a project has fewer eyes, a slower security response, and — in the worst historical cases — an unfamiliar volunteer who is eager to take over publishing rights. That pattern has produced real compromises. If you consume open-source components in anything customer-facing, maintainer health is a signal worth tracking alongside CVE counts: commit cadence, number of active reviewers, whether releases are signed and by whom. Almost nobody tracks it, and it is one of the cheaper additions you can make to a third-party risk process that currently only watches for published vulnerabilities.

Vendor Risk
Directly applies to you

Kimi K3, Qwen 3.8, and Anthropic's (potential) unravelling

An analysis of frontier lab economics argues that aggressive Chinese open-weight releases are compressing margins across the industry, and asks whether the Western labs' current spending can survive the squeeze — Anthropic included.

Treat the headline as a thesis rather than a forecast; predictions of imminent lab collapse have a poor track record, and the piece is arguing from unit economics rather than inside knowledge. But the underlying dynamic is not speculative. When capable open-weight models are released continuously and can be self-hosted, the price a lab can charge for comparable inference is bounded by them. That squeezes the gap between training spend and revenue for anyone whose business is selling access to a proprietary model, and it is a reasonable thing to reason about as a customer rather than dismiss as market noise.

The relevant question for anyone who has built real workflows on a single vendor is not whether the thesis is right. It is what the dependency costs if it is. Model providers are now sitting in the same category as any other critical SaaS supplier — subject to concentration risk, pricing changes, deprecation of the specific model version your prompts were tuned against, and in the extreme, discontinuity. The mitigation is not to hedge everything or to switch on a rumour; it is to know how much rework a forced migration would actually involve, so the answer exists before it is needed.

What to do

Audit where your own automation is coupled to one provider. Identify which workflows depend on vendor-specific interfaces versus portable prompting, keep prompts and orchestration logic in version control separate from any single SDK, and run one meaningful workflow against an alternative model — including a self-hostable open-weight one — so you have a measured estimate of migration cost rather than a guess. Add AI providers to your supplier concentration register if they are not already on it.

AI Governance

China's open-weights AI strategy is winning

The day's most-discussed post argues that American AI has locked itself behind proprietary APIs while Chinese labs published weights, and that the resulting developer gravity is a strategic loss that money cannot easily reverse.

The argument is straightforward: open weights accrue an ecosystem. People fine-tune them, build tooling around them, write papers using them, and deploy them in places an API cannot reach — air-gapped environments, regulated sectors, cost-sensitive products, anywhere the data cannot leave. That accumulated activity is difficult to buy back once it has settled somewhere. Whether this constitutes “winning” depends heavily on what you think the prize is, and the piece is more polemic than analysis on that point, but the observation about developer gravity is hard to argue with when you look at what is actually being built on.

For a security function the interesting consequence is jurisdictional rather than geopolitical. Open weights mean the model can run entirely inside your boundary, which resolves the data residency objection that blocks half of enterprise AI adoption — a genuinely attractive property for a UK or EU organisation navigating GDPR and sectoral rules. It also means provenance and supply chain questions apply to model artefacts the way they apply to any other binary you would not have written yourself: where did the weights come from, what was the training data, what would a backdoored model even look like, and how would you tell. Those are open research questions, and the industry is adopting faster than it is answering them.

Privacy & Ethics

The power of awareness: overcoming surveillance capitalism

A quieter closing argument: that the first and most underrated defence against pervasive commercial surveillance is simply noticing it, and that awareness has to precede any technical or regulatory remedy worth having.

It is an unfashionable position in a field that prefers controls to consciousness, and it pairs instructively with the Flock story earlier in this edition. Councils approved those cameras because nobody in the room had the frame of reference to ask the second question. Regulation follows public attention rather than leading it, and technical countermeasures only get adopted by people who have already understood what they are countering. That ordering is not a reason to wait — it is a reason to treat explanation as part of the work rather than a distraction from it.

There is a version of this that lands squarely in a security leader's remit. Most security awareness programmes teach employees to spot phishing and stop there, treating people as a vulnerability to be patched. A programme that also taught people how data about them is collected, brokered, and inferred would produce better judgement about the tools they adopt, the permissions they grant, and the information they volunteer — and it would make them harder to socially engineer, since pretexting runs on exactly the data this ecosystem sells. It is a more interesting curriculum than the annual compliance module, and a better use of the hour.
