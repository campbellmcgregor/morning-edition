GhostLock: a Stack Use-After-Free Hiding in Every Linux Distro for 15 Years

A newly disclosed use-after-free on the kernel stack — dubbed GhostLock — has apparently sat undetected across every mainstream Linux distribution for fifteen years, reachable by ordinary unprivileged local code.

The nebusec research write-up frames GhostLock as the second act in its "ionstack" series: a subtle lifetime bug where a stack object is freed and then reused along a path the original authors never expected to be reachable. The uncomfortable part is not the cleverness of the exploit but its age. A flaw this old, this widely shipped, and this reachable from unprivileged code is exactly the class of finding that quietly underpins local-privilege-escalation chains — the kind attackers bolt onto a foothold to go from "ran some code" to "owns the box."

For a security leader, the lesson is less about this one CVE and more about the shape of the risk. Fifteen years of exposure means it is baked into container base images, appliances, and long-lived servers that nobody reboots. Patch availability will lag across distros, and embedded or vendor-locked kernels may never get a fix at all. Treat the disclosure as a prompt to check which of your fleet actually receives kernel updates — and which has been quietly accumulating debt.

What to do

Track your distributions' advisories for the GhostLock / ionstack fix and prioritise kernel patching on multi-tenant and internet-adjacent hosts. Inventory anything running an unpatchable or end-of-life kernel — appliances, IoT, frozen container bases — and compensate with tighter local-access controls and monitoring until it can be replaced.

Surveillance & RF

A Cheap Radio That Spots Drones — and Sees Wi-Fi Through Walls

Jeff Geerling wires up QuadRF, a compact software-defined radio array that maps drones overhead and reconstructs the Wi-Fi devices moving on the far side of a solid wall — no cameras, no cooperation from the target.

The demo is the sort of thing that used to require a lab. With off-the-shelf SDR hardware and some signal processing, Geerling shows a rig that passively senses radio energy in the environment: correlating Wi-Fi signals to infer the layout and movement of devices behind an interior wall, and picking drone control links out of the noise. It is a vivid reminder that the radio spectrum is a rich, largely unguarded side channel — one that leaks presence, motion, and device counts to anyone patient enough to listen.

For anyone thinking about physical and operational security, this is the ambient-sensing threat made concrete and cheap. Sensitive spaces are usually hardened against cameras and microphones, not against a neighbour with a passive RF array reading the Wi-Fi bleed-through. The countermeasures are unglamorous — RF-quiet zones, shielding, minimising broadcasting devices in secure areas — but the falling cost of the attack side means the assumption that walls provide privacy is worth retiring.

Abuse & Infrastructure

The Residential-Proxy Problem Is Getting Worse

LWN charts an escalating arms race in which abusive scrapers route through residential proxy networks — frequently stitched together from compromised consumer devices — to bury themselves in ordinary-looking home-broadband traffic.

The piece follows how aggressive scraping, much of it feeding AI training pipelines, has driven site operators to block by IP reputation — and how the scrapers have answered by renting access to vast pools of residential IP addresses. Because that traffic originates from real consumer connections, often on devices whose owners have no idea they are relaying it, the usual defences fail: you cannot simply ban a datacentre range when every request looks like a household in your own market. The result is rising infrastructure cost and a genuine ethical mess about where those proxy endpoints come from.

For defenders this sits at an awkward intersection of abuse, botnet economics, and privacy. The same residential-proxy plumbing that laments-in-passing hammer open-source project servers is also used to launder credential-stuffing and fraud traffic. Blanket blocking risks locking out legitimate users; doing nothing invites cost and abuse. It is a reminder that IP-based trust is eroding, and that behavioural signals and rate-shaping increasingly matter more than allow/deny lists.

Local Inference

Getting GLM 5.2 Running on a Slow Computer

A developer coaxes the GLM 5.2 model into usable speeds on modest hardware with Colibri, an open-source loader that trims memory and quantisation overhead to fit constrained machines.

Colibri is the kind of scrappy project that keeps local AI honest: rather than assuming a rack of GPUs, it wrings acceptable throughput out of a large model on a machine that has no business running it. The write-up walks through the quantisation and memory tricks needed to make GLM 5.2 respond in something better than geological time, and the result is a working setup on hardware most people already own.

The interest here is strategic, not just hobbyist. Every credible path to running capable models locally chips away at the assumption that inference must live in someone else's cloud — which matters for data residency, cost, and resilience. For a security-minded org, on-device or on-prem inference is one answer to the "don't send sensitive prompts to a third party" problem. Projects like Colibri are worth watching precisely because they lower the hardware bar for that option.

Threat Intelligence

How Boko Haram Is Putting Frontier AI to Work

A research report from CASP details how the militant group Boko Haram has begun folding frontier AI models into propaganda, recruitment, and operational planning — an early, sobering field study in adversarial AI use.

The report moves the "terrorists will use AI" conversation from speculation to documented practice. It describes how a non-state armed group has reached for widely available frontier models to scale content production, translate and localise messaging, and support planning tasks that once needed scarce human expertise. None of it requires cutting-edge capability; it requires the same general-purpose tools everyone else now has, applied by people with very different aims.

For a security leader the value is in recalibrating the threat model. The barrier to sophisticated influence operations, social-engineering content, and reconnaissance has fallen for adversaries of every stripe, not just well-resourced states. That argues for taking AI-generated phishing, deepfake pretexting, and automated OSINT seriously as near-term operational risks — and for engaging with the policy debate on model access and abuse safeguards rather than treating it as someone else's problem.

Frugon Finds the LLM Calls You're Overpaying For

Frugon is a local, MIT-licensed tool that inspects your LLM traffic and flags which requests a smaller, cheaper model could have handled just as well — a quiet lever on the runaway inference bills that come with AI-heavy workflows.

The pitch is refreshingly narrow: point Frugon at your LLM usage and it identifies the calls where you are reaching for an expensive frontier model to do work a lighter one would nail. It runs locally, so your prompts and completions do not take a detour through yet another SaaS to be analysed. For anyone whose costs scale with token volume, that targeting is where the real savings live — most of the spend is usually a long tail of routine calls that never needed the top-tier model.

For someone building AI-assisted workflows day to day, this is a practical governance tool as much as a cost one. Knowing which tasks genuinely require the strongest model — and which can be routed to something cheaper, faster, or on-prem — is the difference between a workflow that stays affordable at scale and one that quietly bleeds budget. It also nudges you toward the healthy habit of matching model tier to task difficulty deliberately, rather than defaulting to the biggest hammer.

What to do

Run Frugon against a sample of your Claude Code and Cowork traffic to see where a smaller model would do. Use the findings to set a simple routing rule — cheap model by default, escalate to the frontier model only for genuinely hard tasks — and re-check periodically as your prompt mix shifts.

Legal & IP

Apple Sues OpenAI Over Alleged Trade-Secret Theft

Apple has filed suit against OpenAI, accusing former Apple employees of carrying confidential trade secrets across to the AI lab — a rare, public collision between two of the industry's heaviest names.

The complaint centres on the well-worn but never-solved problem of departing employees and what leaves with them. Apple alleges that ex-staff took proprietary knowledge to OpenAI; OpenAI will contest it. Whatever the courts decide, the case is a marquee example of the single most common way "intellectual property" actually walks out the door — not a dramatic breach, but people, moving between fierce competitors, carrying context in their heads and, allegedly, in their files.

For a security leader this is an insider-risk story wearing a headline's clothes. The controls that matter here are mundane and organisational: clear IP assignment, disciplined offboarding, data-loss monitoring on high-value repositories, and exit processes that actually verify what departing staff can still reach. As the AI talent war intensifies, the movement of people between rivals — and the trade-secret exposure that rides along — is a board-level risk, not just an HR footnote.

Space & Networks

SpaceX Wants 100,000 More Starlink Satellites

SpaceX has asked regulators to bless a hundred thousand additional Starlink satellites, promising roughly a hundredfold jump in constellation bandwidth — and reigniting the debate over orbital congestion and dependence.

The number is staggering on its own terms: a constellation already reshaping global connectivity wants to grow by two orders of magnitude in satellite count. If it happens, rural and hard-to-reach networks get a serious capacity upgrade, and the economics of "connectivity anywhere" shift again. It also sharpens long-running worries about low-Earth-orbit crowding, collision risk, and the astronomy community's complaints about a sky full of moving hardware.

For infrastructure planners there is a quieter strategic thread: resilience and concentration. Starlink is increasingly a piece of critical connectivity for enterprises, remote operations, and even governments — which makes a single commercial constellation a single point of dependence. A hundredfold bandwidth increase is genuinely useful, and worth weighing against the risk of building essential communications on infrastructure you neither own nor control.

Regulation

New York City Moves to Ban Deceptive Subscription Traps

New York City is set to outlaw the dark-pattern subscription tactics that make signing up a single tap and cancelling a maze — one of the more aggressive municipal consumer-protection moves yet.

The proposed rules take direct aim at the "easy in, impossible out" playbook: pre-ticked boxes, buried cancellation flows, and renewals that quietly ratchet up. It follows a broader regulatory turn — from the US FTC's click-to-cancel efforts to EU consumer law — against interfaces engineered to extract consent people did not really give. NYC acting at the city level is notable; it signals that dark patterns are now squarely a legal liability, not just a growth tactic.

For anyone running digital products, the read-across is clear even outside New York: the design choices that pad short-term retention metrics are becoming compliance risks. Consent and cancellation are increasingly held to the same "clear, honest, symmetrical" standard as privacy notices under GDPR. The pragmatic move is to audit your own sign-up and cancellation flows now and make leaving as frictionless as joining — before a regulator makes that decision for you.

Futures & Ethics

AI 2040: A Plan A for the Next Fifteen Years

"AI 2040: Plan A" lays out a deliberately optimistic roadmap for how advanced AI could unfold over the coming fifteen years — a manifesto arguing that the good outcome has to be designed, not assumed.

Where a lot of long-horizon AI writing tilts toward either doom or hand-waving utopia, this piece tries to sketch a concrete "Plan A": the deliberate choices — in governance, deployment, and institutional design — that would have to go right for AI's next fifteen years to land well. It is a normative document more than a prediction, and its value is in naming the decisions rather than pretending the trajectory is inevitable.

Read as a leadership prompt, it is useful even if you disagree with the specifics. The through-line is that optimism without agency is just wishful thinking; good outcomes come from people choosing guardrails, incentives, and defaults on purpose. For anyone whose job now includes AI risk and strategy, that is the right posture — not spectating the future, but taking seriously that the decisions being made this year quietly shape which version of 2040 you get.
