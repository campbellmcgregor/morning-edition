MSI Center: Any Local User Can Grab SYSTEM in Seconds

A researcher shows that MSI's pre-installed Center utility — shipped on millions of gaming laptops and motherboards — lets any logged-in user escalate to full SYSTEM privileges in seconds. No exploit chain, just a trusted service doing the attacker's bidding.

The flaw lives in a privileged component of MSI Center, the tray utility bundled onto a huge slice of MSI laptops and boards. Because a high-privilege service trusts input it should not, an ordinary logged-in user can coax it into running attacker-controlled actions as SYSTEM — the highest authority on a Windows box. There is no memory-corruption artistry here and no social engineering; it is a plain logic failure in software most owners never chose to install and rarely think about, sitting quietly with the run of the machine.

For a CISO the lesson is depressingly familiar: your attack surface includes the OEM bloatware you inherited, not just the software you deployed. Local privilege escalation is the hinge that turns a phished laptop or a limited foothold into full control, and pre-installed vendor tools are a recurring source of it. Anything that runs a privileged helper for the sake of a UI convenience deserves scrutiny, and 'it came with the machine' is not a security posture. Assume the pretty tray icon is a liability until proven otherwise.

What to do

Inventory endpoints for MSI Center and related MSI utilities, especially gaming-grade laptops that crept into the fleet. Remove the software where it is not needed, restrict or disable the privileged service where it is, and track MSI's advisory for a patched build before re-enabling. Treat other OEM tray utilities the same way — assume each is a privilege-escalation candidate until a security review says otherwise.

Surveillance & Privacy

SearXNG Puts a Private Front Door on the Whole Web

SearXNG is a self-hostable metasearch engine that aggregates results from dozens of sources while forwarding none of your identity — no profiling, no cookies, no ad-tech telemetry — trading the polish of Big Search for a query trail that stays yours.

SearXNG is an open-source metasearch engine you run yourself. It takes your query, fans it out to dozens of upstream engines, strips the identifying cruft, and returns aggregated results without building a profile, planting tracking cookies, or feeding an advertising graph. You get much of the reach of mainstream search without becoming the product — and because it is self-hosted, the query log, if one exists at all, is under your control rather than a vendor's. It is search reframed as infrastructure you own instead of a service that owns you.

For privacy-conscious teams this is more than personal hygiene. A shared internal SearXNG instance keeps sensitive research — vulnerability names, acquisition targets, incident keywords — off third-party search infrastructure that logs and monetises everything it sees. The trade-offs are real: you own the upkeep, upstream engines occasionally rate-limit you, and result quality varies with configuration. But for anyone who has watched 'free' search quietly become surveillance, standing up your own front door to the web is a rational, low-cost hedge worth the maintenance.

Serious CVEs Spiked Around a New Frontier Model's Debut

Epoch AI charts a jump in high-severity vulnerability disclosures clustering around the release window of Anthropic's Claude Mythos preview — a correlation, not proof, but one that sharpens the question of whether faster AI-written code is outrunning its own review.

Epoch AI's chart shows a measurable uptick in serious CVE disclosures clustering around the launch of Anthropic's Claude Mythos preview. The authors are careful — this is a correlation over a noisy signal, not a causal indictment of any model — and disclosure counts move for many reasons, from researcher attention to reporting cadence to sheer coincidence. But the shape of the data lands on a nerve the industry has been avoiding: as AI writes more of the world's code, faster, is the review that catches its mistakes keeping pace, or is it being quietly left behind?

The honest answer is that we do not yet know, and that uncertainty is itself the risk. AI-assisted development compresses the time between idea and shipped code — which is exactly the interval where security review normally happens. If generation scales but scrutiny does not, the defect rate need not rise per line for the absolute number of exploitable bugs to climb. This is not an argument against the tools; it is an argument for treating their output with the same suspicion you would give any fast-moving, under-reviewed contributor who never gets tired.

What to do

Treat AI-generated code as untrusted input, not a trusted colleague's work. Keep human review mandatory on anything security-relevant, run SAST and dependency scanning in CI regardless of how the code was written, and measure your own defect-escape rate before and after adopting AI assistance. If you cannot see whether quality is moving, you are flying blind on exactly the metric this data is poking at.

Open Models

Leanstral 1.5 Bets on Proofs, Not Just Predictions

Mistral's Leanstral 1.5 targets formal reasoning — generating machine-checkable proofs at scale — pitching 'proof abundance' as a route to models whose maths, at least, you can verify rather than merely trust.

Leanstral 1.5 is Mistral's push into formal reasoning: a model tuned to produce machine-checkable proofs rather than confident prose. The pitch — 'proof abundance for all' — is that if a system can generate proofs cheaply and a proof checker can verify them mechanically, you get a rare thing in the AI world: output you can trust because it has been checked, not because the model sounded sure of itself.

That verification story is the interesting part for security people, who are chronically allergic to 'trust me.' A model whose mathematical claims are independently checkable is a different risk object than one whose answers you must spot-audit by hand. The near-term uses are narrow — formal maths, verified algorithms, parts of protocol design — but the direction matters. The frontier that pays off for defenders is not the model that is most fluent; it is the one whose work you can prove.

Threat Modelling

Soatok's Plain-Language Guide to Threat Models

Security researcher Soatok lays out threat modelling without the ceremony: who is your adversary, what can they do, and what are you actually protecting — a reminder that most breaches start with a threat model nobody bothered to write down.

Threat modelling has a reputation for ceremony — matrices, frameworks, day-long workshops that produce a document nobody reads. Soatok's guide cuts against that. The core is three plain questions: who is your adversary, what can they actually do, and what are you truly trying to protect? Most real-world compromises, the piece argues, trace back to a threat model that was either wrong or never written — a defender guarding the wrong door while the adversary strolls through another one entirely.

The value for a security leader is in the reframing. A threat model is not a compliance artefact; it is the assumption set every other control silently depends on. Encrypting data at rest is pointless if your real adversary is an insider with legitimate access. Get the model wrong and you spend budget hardening against threats you do not face while ignoring the one that lands. Written plainly and revisited often, it is the cheapest security investment most teams still manage to skip.

AI Economics

Inference Keeps Getting Cheaper — Faster Than Expected

Benchmarks running GLM-5.2 on AMD silicon show performance-per-dollar still improving steeply, undercutting the assumption that frontier-class inference stays expensive — and reshaping the build-versus-buy maths for anyone budgeting AI into a product.

Benchmarks pairing GLM-5.2 with AMD hardware show performance-per-dollar on inference still climbing steeply — cheaper tokens, faster throughput, on silicon that is not Nvidia's. The headline is not a single number but a trend line: the assumption that frontier-class inference must stay expensive is aging badly, and each drop resets the economics of building AI into a product rather than renting it by the call.

For anyone budgeting AI, this is the variable that quietly moves everything else. Falling inference cost turns features that were too pricey to ship into obvious wins, and it strengthens the case for open-weight models running on commodity or non-Nvidia hardware instead of metered APIs. The security and sovereignty upside is real too: cheaper self-hosted inference means sensitive data need not leave your perimeter to get the benefit. The build-versus-buy line keeps sliding, steadily, toward build.

Abuse & Defence

Inside the Machinery Reddit Uses to Fight Spam

A rare look under the hood at Reddit's anti-spam stack — the signals, heuristics, and feedback loops that keep a firehose of automated abuse at bay — and a useful mirror for anyone defending a platform against the same brutal economics.

Platform anti-spam is usually a black box, so this walk through Reddit's internals is a treat. It sketches the layered reality of the problem: cheap, relentless automated abuse on one side, and on the other a stack of signals, heuristics, reputation scores, and human-in-the-loop feedback that together keep the firehose survivable. Nothing is a silver bullet; the defence works because it is many mediocre filters stacked into something an attacker cannot cheaply route around, degrading gracefully instead of failing all at once.

The transferable lesson is economic. Spam is an arbitrage: it pays as long as the cost of getting through stays below the return. Effective defence does not aim for perfection — it aims to raise the attacker's cost faster than they can raise their throughput. That framing applies well beyond social platforms, to account fraud, credential stuffing, and the automated abuse every internet-facing service now absorbs. Read it as a case study in defending against adversaries who scale cheaply.

Field Notes on Coding With Agents, From an Island

Dan Luu writes up what agentic coding actually feels like in practice — the loops, the babysitting, the places where the model shines and the places it quietly wastes your afternoon — grounded, unhyped, and rare for exactly that.

Dan Luu's notes on agentic coding are refreshingly free of both hype and reflexive scorn. Writing from, of all places, the Galápagos, he catalogues what the work actually feels like: the loops where an agent grinds usefully toward a goal, the moments it confidently walks off a cliff, and the babysitting overhead the demos never show. It is the rare account that treats the tools as tools — powerful, uneven, and worth understanding precisely rather than believing or dismissing on faith.

For anyone building AI-assisted workflows, the value is in the texture. Luu is specific about where agentic loops pay off and where they quietly waste an afternoon, which is exactly the knowledge you cannot get from a launch post. The practical takeaway is that leverage comes from designing the loop — clear goals, tight feedback, guardrails on the failure modes — rather than from trusting the model to freewheel. Read it as engineering notes from someone keeping score, not as evangelism.

What to do

If you run agentic coding day to day, mine this for concrete loop patterns: where to insert verification steps, how to bound an agent's autonomy, and which tasks to hand off versus keep. The failure modes Luu names are the ones worth building guardrails around in your own Claude Code setups before they cost you a quiet afternoon.

Connectivity & Power

Africa's Quiet Pivot to Starlink

Across the continent, users and small ISPs are turning to Starlink where terrestrial networks fail — buying reliable bandwidth, but also handing a single foreign operator growing leverage over how millions of people get online.

Across much of Africa, where terrestrial broadband is patchy, expensive, or simply absent, users and small ISPs are turning to Starlink for bandwidth that actually works. The Economist frames it as a genuine leap in access — schools, businesses, and remote clinics getting online where the incumbents never reached. The demand is real and the connectivity is transformative in ways that are easy to underrate from a place already wired with fibre and 5G.

The uncomfortable half of the story is dependency. Reliable access now flows through a single foreign operator with its own commercial and political priorities, largely outside any one government's regulatory reach. That is leverage — over pricing, over availability, potentially over who gets to communicate during a crisis. It is the same concentration-risk pattern security leaders know from cloud and supply chains, playing out at the scale of national infrastructure. Connectivity gained; sovereignty quietly mortgaged in the same transaction.

AI & Ethics

Barred From an AI During a Job Talk

An academic argues that being forbidden to use a chatbot during a hiring 'chalk talk' amounts to discrimination — a provocative claim that forces an uncomfortable question about which cognitive tools we now treat as part of the person.

The essay makes a deliberately provocative claim: being forbidden to use a chatbot during an academic 'chalk talk' job presentation is a form of discrimination. The argument runs that for some people AI assistance is not a shortcut but an accommodation — a way of working now inseparable from how they think — and that banning it tests the candidate on a disability rather than on the science they were invited to present. It is easy to scoff at, and easy to feel the pull of anyway.

It is worth sitting with. The line between a tool and a crutch, between an accommodation and an unfair advantage, is exactly the boundary every institution is now being forced to draw — in hiring, in exams, in the workplace. Whatever you make of this particular case, the underlying question is not going away: as AI folds into how people think and produce, on what principled basis do we ever say 'not here'? The comfortable answers are quietly running out.
