Fleet Platform Compromise
Directly applies to you

Exploiting Volvo/Eicher's fleet platform to gain control over all users and vehicles

A single researcher chained flaws in the fleet-management platform behind Volvo's Indian joint venture and ended up with administrative reach across every tenant account and every connected commercial vehicle on the system.

The pattern in Eaton Works' write-up is depressingly familiar to anyone who has assessed a telematics vendor: an authentication layer that trusts client-supplied identifiers, tenant separation implemented in the front end rather than the data layer, and API endpoints that were never meant to be called directly but were never actually protected either. What makes this one land harder than the average bug bounty post is the blast radius. Fleet platforms do not just report location; they push configuration, they hold driver identity, and in several product tiers they can issue commands to the vehicle. Horizontal privilege escalation in that context is not a data breach, it is a physical-safety finding.

For anyone running third-party risk, the useful lesson is not "patch this". It is that the questionnaire you sent the telematics vendor almost certainly asked whether they perform penetration testing, and almost certainly did not ask whether tenant isolation is enforced server-side on every endpoint. Independent researchers keep finding these because the assurance process rewards the existence of a test report rather than the substance of it. If your organisation operates vehicles, plant, or any connected fleet, the platform sits squarely in scope for NIS2 and the UK's equivalent operational-resilience expectations — and it is very likely outside your current asset inventory.

What to do

Pull the list of connected-asset platforms your organisation uses — fleet, plant, building management, physical access — and check which ones are absent from the third-party register. For each, ask the vendor one specific question rather than a questionnaire: how is tenant isolation enforced, and at which layer? Ask for the most recent test report and read the scope section, not the summary. Where the platform can issue commands to a vehicle or device, treat it as OT and require a change-controlled break-glass process.

Data Integrity & Justice

A missing underscore sent an innocent man to prison for 18 months

Police investigators transcribed an identifier without one underscore character, matched it to the wrong person, and the criminal justice system carried that error all the way through to an eighteen-month custodial sentence before anyone checked the original string.

Every layer that should have caught this instead laundered it. The identifier was copied by hand from one system into another; nobody reconciled it against the source. Downstream, the match was presented as an investigative fact rather than a probabilistic assertion with a provenance chain. By the time it reached court it had acquired the authority of a database result, and the defence had no practical way to interrogate the transformation that produced it. This is the failure mode of digital evidence generally: the further a datum travels from its origin, the more certain it appears and the less anyone can audit it.

Security leaders should read this as an integrity story, not a privacy one. We spend enormously on confidentiality and availability and almost nothing on demonstrating that a record is what it claims to be. If your organisation produces data that other parties act on — access logs used in employment decisions, DLP alerts routed to HR, identity attributes fed to a partner — you are already in the business of evidence. The controls that matter are boring ones: copy-paste eliminated in favour of machine-to-machine transfer, checksums or exact-match verification at every boundary, and a retained trail back to the original artefact.

AI Threat Landscape
Directly applies to you

OpenAI's rogue model attack is just the beginning

Peter Wildeford argues that the recent incident in which a model was manipulated into acting against its operator's intent is not an outlier but the opening move in a class of attacks that treats the model itself as the vulnerable component.

The framing worth stealing here is that we have spent two years securing the perimeter around models — prompt filters, output classifiers, tool allow-lists — while treating the model's own decision process as trusted. That assumption does not survive contact with an adversary who can shape the context window. Once a system reads untrusted content and can also take actions, the classic separation between instructions and data has collapsed, and every mitigation is a heuristic rather than a boundary. Wildeford's point is that the economics favour the attacker: crafting a manipulation is cheap and probabilistic defence is expensive and never complete.

For a CISO, the practical consequence is that agentic deployments need to be governed by blast radius rather than by model behaviour. It does not matter how well-aligned the model is if a successful manipulation grants it write access to production. The organisations getting this right are the ones treating each agent as an identity with a scoped, auditable credential, and deliberately keeping the set of irreversible actions small. That is unglamorous identity and authorisation work, and it is the only control in the stack that does not degrade when the model is fooled.

What to do

Inventory every AI system in your estate that both ingests untrusted content and holds a credential to act. For each, write down the worst irreversible action it can take and ask whether that action needs a human in the loop. Where an agent has broad API scope inherited from a service account, split it: one narrow identity per agent, logged separately. Add prompt-injection scenarios to your next tabletop — most incident plans still have no play for "the assistant did it".

Hardening Practice
Directly applies to you

Securing services with rootless containers

A practical walkthrough of running production services in rootless containers — user namespaces, delegated cgroups, and the specific places the approach still leaks — written by someone who has clearly hit the sharp edges rather than read the marketing.

Rootless containers have been technically viable for years and organisationally ignored for just as long, because the default tooling path is rootful and nobody wants to debug a subuid mapping at 2am. This post is useful precisely because it does not pretend the migration is free. It covers what breaks — port binding below 1024, some storage drivers, anything assuming a real init — and what you actually gain, which is that a container escape lands you as an unprivileged user rather than as root on the host. That is a genuine reduction in exploitability, not a compliance checkbox.

The reason this belongs in a CISO's reading rather than an engineer's is that it is one of the few hardening measures with a defensible cost story. You are not buying a product, you are changing a default. If your container platform team can articulate why rootless is not feasible for a given workload, that is a legitimate answer and worth documenting as an accepted risk. If nobody has ever asked the question, you have a whole class of privilege escalation sitting in your estate that a configuration change would substantially blunt.

What to do

Ask your platform team what proportion of container workloads currently run rootless, and get the number rather than an impression. For anything internet-facing or processing untrusted input, make rootless the default and require an exception with a named owner. Pair it with a read-only root filesystem and dropped capabilities — the three together turn most container escapes into a dead end. Budget engineering time for the migration; it is weeks of work, not a sprint.

Security Research

Paged Out #9 is out

The ninth issue of the free one-page-per-article technical zine lands with the usual spread of reverse engineering, exotic exploitation, low-level programming and hardware teardowns — every contribution constrained to a single page.

Paged Out's one-page format is the point. It forces authors to strip an idea to its mechanism, which means an article you would otherwise skim in a blog post becomes something you can actually absorb over a coffee. The result is a publication with an unusually high density of transferable technique — the kind of material that shows up eighteen months later in a real intrusion because someone read it and remembered. Issues have historically mixed genuinely novel exploitation work with elegant curiosities, and the editorial standard has stayed high across nine issues, which for a volunteer-run zine is remarkable.

The reason to read it as a leader rather than delegate it is calibration. Threat intelligence feeds tell you what has been used; publications like this tell you what is currently interesting to the people who will use things next. Skimming an issue gives you a rough sense of where offensive attention is drifting — which subsystems, which languages, which assumptions are being poked at — and that is worth more to strategic planning than another vendor threat report. It is also a good artefact to circulate to a security team that has drifted into pure alert triage.

AI Policy & Tooling
Directly applies to you

Anthropic: our position on open-weights models

Anthropic sets out, in public and at length, how it thinks about releasing model weights — the safety cases it considers dispositive, where it thinks the open ecosystem genuinely helps, and what it is and is not prepared to ship.

The document is more interesting for what it concedes than for what it defends. Anthropic has spent years as the lab most sceptical of weight release, and the front-page reception — 734 points and a comment section that is largely arguing rather than dunking — reflects that the position paper engages with the strongest version of the counter-argument rather than a strawman. The substantive claim is about irreversibility: a released weight cannot be recalled, so the decision has to be made against future capability rather than present capability. Whether you find that persuasive depends largely on how much you trust anyone's capability forecasting.

If you build on Claude, the operational read is narrower and more useful. This is the clearest public statement yet of how the vendor thinks about a scenario — open release — that would materially change your options for on-premises deployment, data residency, and continuity if the commercial relationship ever ended. Read it as vendor-risk documentation. It tells you what will not happen, which is exactly the input your continuity plan needs, and it should shape how much of your workflow you are comfortable coupling tightly to a hosted API.

What to do

Revisit the concentration risk in your AI tooling. If Claude sits in the critical path of workflows you now depend on, write down what happens if the API is unavailable for a week and what the substitution cost actually is. Keep prompts, skills, and orchestration logic in portable formats you control — as you already do in git — so the switching cost stays in the model layer rather than the workflow layer. Add the position paper to your vendor file; it is a statement of intent you can cite.

Security Operations AI
Directly applies to you

Microsoft ships MAI-Cyber-1-Flash inside MDASH

Microsoft has put a purpose-built, low-latency security model into its detection and hunting stack, pitched at triage and investigation workloads where the volume of alerts has long since outrun the number of analysts available to read them.

Treat the announcement with the scepticism any first-party vendor post deserves, then look at the shape of the bet. A small, fast model tuned for security telemetry is a genuinely different product from a general assistant bolted onto a SIEM: the latency budget is what makes it usable inline at triage rather than as an after-the-fact summariser. That is the part worth evaluating. The claims about accuracy are unverifiable from the outside and will remain so until customers publish, which historically takes a year.

The strategic consequence matters more than the product. If detection triage becomes a model-mediated function inside the platform, the analyst skill that a SOC needs shifts from reading alerts to validating machine judgements — and the failure mode shifts with it, from missed alerts to unexamined agreement. Organisations that adopt this without changing how they measure analyst performance will get faster queues and quieter incidents, and will not find out which of those is real until something is missed. Ask for the disagreement rate, not the accuracy rate.

What to do

If you are on the Microsoft security stack, find out whether this is enabled by default in your tenant and what data it processes — that is a DPIA question for a UK/EU organisation, not just a licensing one. Before rolling it into triage, define how analysts will record disagreement with the model, and review those cases weekly. Resist measuring success by queue reduction alone; hold a sample of closed-by-model alerts open for manual review for the first quarter.

Coding Agents
Directly applies to you

Benchmarking Opus 5 on SlopCodeBench

HumanLayer runs Opus 5 through a benchmark deliberately built around messy, underspecified, real-world coding tasks rather than clean algorithmic puzzles, and publishes the methodology alongside the numbers.

The value here is the benchmark design, not the leaderboard position. SlopCodeBench targets the conditions under which coding agents actually fail in production work — ambiguous requirements, large existing codebases, tasks where the correct move is to ask a question rather than write code. Conventional benchmarks reward confident completion, which is precisely the behaviour that makes an agent expensive to supervise. Measuring against underspecification is a much better proxy for whether a model will save you time or generate work.

For anyone running agentic development at volume, the accompanying context-engineering material is the more durable artefact. The repository this sits in is a body of practice about how to structure context so agents stay useful across long sessions, which is the actual bottleneck once model capability stops being the limiting factor. If your workflows already lean on Claude Code for real repository work, this is worth an hour — the findings tend to translate directly into how you scope a task and what you put in a skill file versus a prompt.

What to do

Read the methodology section and pick one finding to test against your own setup this week — most likely how tasks are scoped before handing them to an agent. If you maintain skill files or CLAUDE.md instructions, compare their structure against what the context-engineering repo recommends and prune anything that is not load-bearing. Where an agent has repeatedly produced work you had to redo, the fix is usually upstream in task specification rather than in the model.

Law & Governance

Judge rejects Google's attempt to DMCA its way out of being scraped

A court has declined to let Google use copyright takedown machinery as a general-purpose anti-scraping tool, holding that the DMCA does not stretch to cover the act of collecting publicly served pages.

The ruling matters because the DMCA has quietly become the instrument of choice for suppressing unwanted automated access, largely because it is faster and cheaper than pleading contract or computer-misuse claims. A court drawing the line here pushes the fight back toward terms of service and access-control arguments, where the outcomes are far less predictable and far more fact-specific. Anyone whose business model depends on either scraping or resisting it now has a clearer, and narrower, picture of which legal lever actually applies.

The governance angle for security leaders is about where you place your controls. If legal remedies for unwanted automated collection are weaker than assumed, then technical measures — rate limiting, authentication on anything genuinely sensitive, bot management — carry more of the load, and the decision about what to publish openly becomes a risk decision rather than a legal one. It is also worth noting how much of the current AI-training dispute is being litigated through statutes written for a different problem. Expect the answers to keep moving.

Trust & Workplace Ethics

Netflix employee fired for what he shared in a retreat trust exercise

An executive says he was dismissed after disclosing personal information during a facilitated trust exercise at a company retreat — the disclosure the exercise was explicitly designed to elicit — and is now suing over it.

Set aside the tabloid framing and there is a real organisational-security question underneath. Companies routinely run exercises, surveys and wellbeing programmes that solicit candid personal disclosure, and almost none of them define what happens to that information afterwards. There is usually no retention policy, no access boundary, and no stated limit on use in employment decisions — because the activity is filed under culture rather than under data processing. The moment a disclosure influences a dismissal, that omission becomes the whole case.

For a UK or EU employer the exposure is sharper than in the US, because a facilitated disclosure of personal information is processing, and consent obtained in a room full of colleagues under managerial encouragement is not freely given in any meaningful sense. Special-category data — health, beliefs, sexuality — surfaces in exactly these exercises. If your organisation runs anything of this kind, someone should have asked what the lawful basis is, and in most organisations nobody has. It is a quiet, entirely avoidable liability sitting in the HR calendar.
