Critical Vulnerability
Directly applies to you

FortiSandbox command injection lands on CISA's must-patch list

CVE-2026-25089 lets an unauthenticated attacker run arbitrary OS commands on FortiSandbox appliances, and its addition to CISA's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalogue means it is not theoretical — it is being used in the wild right now.

FortiSandbox sits in the trust-sensitive part of a network: it detonates suspicious files and feeds verdicts back to firewalls and mail gateways. A pre-auth command-injection flaw in that box is close to worst-case, because the appliance is often exposed to internal traffic and sometimes to management interfaces that were never meant to face anything hostile. An attacker who lands code there inherits a privileged vantage point over exactly the flows a security team relies on to catch malware.

The KEV listing is the part that should move this to the top of your queue. CISA only adds entries when it has evidence of active exploitation, and for federal agencies the listing carries a hard remediation deadline. Fortinet appliance bugs have a long history of being chained into ransomware intrusions, so treat the KEV flag as a countdown rather than a suggestion. If you cannot patch immediately, the exposure of the management plane is the thing to strangle first.

What to do

Inventory every FortiSandbox instance today and confirm the fixed firmware version against Fortinet's advisory before the weekend. Restrict the admin and API interfaces to a management VLAN or jump host, pull them off any internet-facing path, and review appliance logs for unexpected command execution. If you run managed detection, ask your provider whether they are hunting for this specific CVE now.

Surveillance & Privacy

A hardened phone becomes a lifeline for abuse survivors

An Australian privacy advocacy group is now recommending GrapheneOS devices to domestic-violence survivors, on the argument that a stalkerware-resistant phone is one of the few technical controls that meaningfully shifts power back to the person being tracked.

The threat model here is unusually personal. An abuser often has physical access, knows the passwords, and may have installed monitoring apps under the guise of a shared family plan. Mainstream phones leak location and messaging metadata to a sprawl of apps and cloud accounts that are hard to fully audit, and the account-recovery flows that keep ordinary users safe become attack surface when the adversary sits at the kitchen table. GrapheneOS strips much of that back, sandboxes Google services, and makes covert surveillance software far harder to hide.

For a security leader, the interesting part is the reframing of hardening as a safety intervention rather than a compliance checkbox. The same properties that matter to a journalist or an executive under targeted threat — verified boot, minimal telemetry, granular permissions — turn out to matter most to the most vulnerable users. It is a useful reminder that privacy engineering is not an abstract virtue; for some people it is the difference between being findable and being safe.

Data Protection
Directly applies to you

The intimate data leaking out of your period tracker

A BBC investigation finds that popular menstruation-tracking apps quietly share deeply personal details — cycles, sexual activity, pregnancy intentions — with third parties, exposing a category of special-category data that most users never imagined was being brokered.

Reproductive-health data is about as sensitive as personal data gets, and under UK GDPR it falls squarely into special category territory that demands explicit consent and tight handling. The BBC's reporting shows a gap between the reassuring app-store copy and the actual data flows, where inference-rich signals leak to advertisers and analytics vendors. In a post-Dobbs world the stakes are not hypothetical: the same data that personalises an app can be subpoenaed, sold, or breached.

The lesson for a CISO reaches well beyond femtech. Any product that touches health, family, or biometric signals is handling data that regulators and journalists now scrutinise aggressively, and "we only share with partners" is no longer a defensible posture. The reputational and regulatory downside of a special-category data leak dwarfs the ad revenue it funds, and third-party SDKs are where that risk usually hides.

What to do

If your organisation ships or embeds any app handling health, wellbeing, or family data, commission a data-flow audit of every third-party SDK and confirm your lawful basis for special-category processing. Check that consent is genuinely explicit and revocable, and that your DPIA reflects what the app actually transmits — not what the privacy policy claims. Treat any inference about health status as regulated data.

AI Tooling

LM Studio Bionic puts an agent on top of local models

LM Studio's new Bionic release adds an agentic layer that lets open-weight models run tools, browse files, and chain multi-step tasks entirely on your own hardware — no cloud API, no data leaving the machine.

The pitch is straightforward: the agent patterns that made hosted assistants useful — tool calling, retrieval, iterative planning — arrive for models you download and run offline. For anyone who has been reluctant to pipe sensitive material through a third-party endpoint, that is a meaningful shift. A local agent that can read your files and execute tools keeps the whole loop inside your trust boundary, which is exactly the constraint many regulated teams operate under.

The security caveat is the same one that applies to every agent: capability is risk. An offline model with filesystem and tool access is a smaller data-exfiltration problem but a larger local-blast-radius one, so sandboxing, scoped permissions, and careful tool allow-listing still matter. Local does not mean safe by default — it means the failure modes move from "leaked to a vendor" to "did something unexpected on my box." Worth a look for private prototyping.

AI Agents

The human-in-the-loop is tired

Pydantic's team argues that the reflexive answer to AI risk — keep a human reviewing every action — quietly breaks down as agents scale, because a person rubber-stamping a hundred decisions an hour is neither meaningful oversight nor a real control.

The essay names a discomfort many practitioners feel but rarely articulate. "Human in the loop" is invoked as a safety guarantee, yet in practice it often means a fatigued reviewer clicking approve on output they cannot realistically verify at speed. The oversight is theatre: it satisfies a governance checkbox while providing little of the assurance the checkbox implies. As agents take on higher volumes of lower-stakes actions, the human becomes a bottleneck that is bypassed in spirit even when it is preserved on paper.

The constructive turn is to design controls that match the actual work. That means reserving human judgement for genuinely consequential decisions, and everywhere else replacing sign-off with guardrails that hold without a person watching — typed constraints, policy checks, reversible actions, and audit trails that make bad outcomes detectable and recoverable. For a security leader deploying agents internally, this reframes the question from "is a human watching?" to "what happens when no one is?" That is the more honest design brief.

AI Models

Kimi K3 nudges the open frontier higher

Moonshot's Kimi K3 arrives as an open-weight model pitched at genuine frontier performance, topping Hacker News with a scale of benchmark claims that would have read as flagship-lab territory only a year ago.

The steady compression of the gap between closed frontier labs and open releases is one of the defining trends of the year, and K3 is the latest data point. Each such release lowers the cost of capable AI and widens who can run it without a vendor contract — attractive for teams that want control, sovereignty, or air-gapped deployment. For organisations wary of sending data to a US hyperscaler, a strong open model that runs on your own infrastructure is a materially different proposition.

It also sharpens the governance questions. Open weights mean the model can be fine-tuned, jailbroken, and embedded without oversight, and provenance of a Chinese-origin model matters for some regulated buyers. The right posture is neither hype nor reflexive suspicion: evaluate it on your own tasks, understand the licence terms, and treat "open" as a supply-chain fact to be assessed rather than a badge of trust. The ceiling moved again; the diligence does not change.

Digital Ownership

Sony deletes more films from libraries people 'bought'

Sony has again removed purchased movies from customers' digital libraries, a fresh reminder that the "buy" button on a streaming platform confers a revocable licence, not the ownership the word implies.

The pattern is now familiar and no less galling for it: content that customers paid to own vanishes when a licensing deal lapses, with the platform pointing to terms of service that quietly reserve the right to revoke access. The gap between the consumer's mental model — a purchase is permanent — and the legal reality of a licence is the whole story, and it keeps recurring because the incentives for platforms have not changed.

For anyone responsible for data and vendor strategy, the lesson generalises well beyond entertainment. Anything you access through a provider's platform — media, SaaS data, cloud-hosted archives — is subject to their continuity, their business decisions, and their terms. "It is in the cloud" is not the same as "it is yours," and the only durable hedge is exportability: keep local copies of what genuinely matters, and read the revocation clauses before you rely on continued access.

AI Tools

NotebookLM becomes Gemini Notebook

Google has folded NotebookLM into its Gemini brand, renaming the research-and-synthesis tool to Gemini Notebook and signalling a tighter integration with the rest of its AI stack.

NotebookLM earned a genuine following as a source-grounded research assistant: point it at your own documents and it summarises, cross-references, and answers with citations back to the material. The rebrand to Gemini Notebook is mostly a consolidation play, bringing a well-liked product under the umbrella Google is betting the company on and, presumably, wiring it more tightly to Gemini's models and account plumbing.

For users the substance matters more than the name. The value was always the grounding — answers anchored to your sources rather than the open-web hallucination surface — and the question is whether that discipline survives the integration or gets diluted into a general chatbot. For teams weighing it as a knowledge tool, the enterprise data-handling terms are the thing to read closely: where your uploaded documents live, how they are retained, and whether they train anything are the details that decide if it is safe for real work.

AI & Antitrust

Google ordered to open Android to AI rivals

A court has ordered Google to give competing AI providers more access on Android smartphones, the latest antitrust intervention aimed at loosening the grip default placement gives incumbents over how a billion people first meet AI.

Defaults are destiny in consumer software, and the assistant baked into a phone captures enormous behavioural advantage before a user ever considers an alternative. The ruling attacks exactly that chokepoint, forcing Google to let rival AI models reach Android users rather than funnelling everyone into its own Gemini stack. It echoes the search-distribution fights of the past decade, now replayed for the AI era where the stakes are arguably higher.

For a security and technology leader the second-order effects are the interesting ones. More AI providers reaching devices by default means a wider, more fragmented supply chain of models with access to user context — and a correspondingly wider set of data-handling and trust questions for anyone managing a fleet. Competition is healthy, but "more choice on the device" also means more vendors to vet, more SDKs to inventory, and more defaults to review before they are quietly set for your users.

AI Ethics

The LLM critics are right. I use LLMs anyway

A developer writes an unusually honest essay conceding the critics' case — the environmental cost, the training-data ethics, the hype — and then explains why he keeps reaching for the tools regardless, refusing the comfort of a clean answer.

What makes the piece worth reading is that it declines to resolve its own tension. The author does not dismiss the critiques as noise, nor does he perform the guilty-then-absolved arc that so much AI writing follows. He holds both truths at once: the objections are largely valid, and the productivity gains are real enough that abstaining would be its own kind of pretence. That refusal to flatten the contradiction is rarer than it should be in this debate.

For anyone leading an organisation through AI adoption, this is the more mature register to model. The choice is rarely between virtuous abstinence and uncritical embrace; it is about using powerful, ethically-complicated tools with eyes open — naming the costs, mitigating what you can, and being honest that trade-offs remain. Pretending the tension away, in either direction, is the failure mode. Sitting with it is the adult position.
