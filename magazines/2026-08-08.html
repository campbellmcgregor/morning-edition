Critical Infrastructure
Directly applies to you

Water system controllers don't belong on the internet, says ex-NSA chief

After a run of suspected Iranian intrusions at US water utilities, a former NSA director has offered the least fashionable and most effective security advice available: take the programmable logic controllers off the public internet entirely, and stop pretending remote convenience is worth the exposure.

The uncomfortable part of every water-sector incident is how little sophistication it requires. Small utilities run a handful of HMIs and PLCs, frequently reachable straight off the public internet with default or shared credentials, because a two-person operations team needs to check a pump at two in the morning without driving to the site. Attackers do not need a zero-day for this; they need a search engine for exposed devices and some patience. When a former NSA chief says these things should not be internet-facing, he is not sketching an aspirational architecture — he is naming the single control that would have prevented most publicly reported water intrusions of the past three years.

For anyone outside the water sector, the transferable lesson is about a class of asset rather than a sector. Every organisation has an equivalent: building management, cooling in a data hall, physical access controllers, a lab instrument, a legacy vendor appliance with a forgotten web interface. These sit outside the asset inventory, outside patch cycles and outside the identity provider, which is precisely why they are such a dependable route in. UK critical national infrastructure operators carry NIS-derived obligations here, and regulators have grown noticeably less patient with "the vendor requires inbound remote access" as an explanation.

What to do

Run an external-facing discovery pass this week that looks for operational-technology and building-systems protocols rather than only web and mail — Modbus, S7, BACnet, VNC and vendor-specific management ports. Anything exposed should move behind a broker or VPN with MFA before month end, and any supplier contract that mandates direct inbound access goes on the list for renegotiation at renewal. Then ask who owns the OT asset inventory, by name; if the answer takes longer than one sentence, that is itself the finding.

Bots & The Open Web

A year of fighting scrapers on my 1.5 million-page website

A site operator publishes twelve months of traffic data showing that roughly ninety-nine percent of requests came from automated crawlers rather than readers, and documents what it costs to serve an audience that is mostly machines harvesting content for somebody else's model.

The number is the story. When a site of any real size instruments its logs honestly, the human share of traffic turns out to be a rounding error, and the remainder is a mixture of declared AI crawlers, undeclared ones rotating through residential proxies, and ordinary scrapers that ignore robots.txt because nothing enforces it. This account is valuable because it is empirical rather than ideological: bandwidth bills, cache-hit ratios, and the specific evasion behaviours that appeared once blocking began. It reads less like a manifesto about artificial intelligence and more like a year-long incident report about infrastructure abuse.

Security teams should read it as a detection and capacity problem, not a copyright one. If ninety-nine percent of inbound requests are automated, then WAF thresholds, rate limits, anomaly baselines and volumetric alerting are all calibrated against noise, and a genuine credential-stuffing run or reconnaissance sweep hides very comfortably inside that noise. The same residential-proxy infrastructure that harvests content is rented by the hour to people doing worse things. Anyone who has tried to separate the two from logs alone knows the honest answer is that mostly you cannot, which is why per-identity limits and behavioural signals outperform IP reputation.

AI Threat Capability
Directly applies to you

Responding to the next frontier of critical cyber capabilities

OpenAI sets out its position on frontier models crossing into genuinely useful offensive-security capability, describing the safeguards, access restrictions and disclosure posture it intends to apply as those same models become materially better at finding and exploiting software flaws.

Strip away the diplomacy and this is a frontier lab stating that its models are approaching, or have reached, the point where vulnerability discovery and exploitation stop being a party trick. That claim deserves neither dismissal nor panic. The defensive upside is real and already visible in bug-bounty triage, fuzzing harness generation and patch diffing. The awkward downside is that the same capability compresses the interval between a patch appearing and a working exploit existing — and that interval is the load-bearing assumption underneath every vulnerability-management programme, including yours, usually without anyone having written it down.

What a document like this really offers a security leader is leverage rather than information. It is a citable, first-party statement from a model developer that offensive capability is scaling, which is exactly the artefact you need when arguing for a shorter patch SLA or for exposure-management funding a board previously judged adequate. Treat the described safeguards as best-effort and non-durable: access tiers leak, weights get replicated, and open-weight models now trail the frontier by months rather than years. Plan on the assumption that the capability becomes generally available, not that it stays gated behind somebody's policy.

What to do

Take your current patch SLAs for internet-facing systems to the next risk committee and ask explicitly whether thirty days still holds in an environment where exploit development is assisted rather than artisanal. Pair the question with one concrete defensive pilot — an AI-assisted triage pass over your own pen-test or bug-bounty backlog — so the discussion carries evidence from your estate rather than a vendor's blog post. Write down the assumption you are now making about exploit-development speed, so the decision can be reviewed rather than re-argued.

Agent Infrastructure

Kitesurf: an agent-first browser that runs in V8 isolates

Cloudflare introduces a browser designed for AI agents rather than people, running pages inside V8 isolates instead of full browser processes, with the explicit goal of making automated navigation cheap and fast enough to run at platform scale.

The interesting decision here is the isolate boundary. Conventional headless automation means a full Chromium per session: heavy, slow to start, and awkward to sandbox convincingly. Running agent sessions in V8 isolates trades browser fidelity for density and startup latency, which is the right trade when the consumer is a model rather than a human eye. Expect gaps around complex rendering, media and extension behaviour, and expect those gaps to matter less each quarter as agent traffic becomes the dominant automated workload on the web.

The security question is not whether the isolate holds. It is what the agent is permitted to touch. An agent browser is a confused-deputy machine by construction: it reads untrusted page content, then takes actions using credentials you handed it. Prompt injection embedded in a page becomes a privileged action, and conventional egress monitoring sees only legitimate outbound HTTPS to a legitimate service. Anyone evaluating agentic browsing should be asking about credential scoping, per-action approval and audit trails long before asking about rendering fidelity or price per page.

Low-Level Research

Assembly Hall of Shame

A catalogue of instruction-set design decisions that have aged badly — surprising encodings, ambiguous semantics and undocumented corners of assembly languages that reliably produce bugs, and occasionally produce exploitable ones.

There is a specific pleasure in reading a well-kept list of other people's architectural regrets, and a specific professional value in it too. The collection gathers instructions whose behaviour diverges from their documentation, encodings that overlap in ways disassemblers get wrong, and flag semantics that only make sense once you know which decade the silicon shipped in. The curator is the researcher behind sandsifter and a substantial body of work on undocumented processor behaviour, so the entries come from measurement rather than folklore — which is what separates this from the usual list of programming grievances.

For a defender, the practical thread is tooling trust. Every disassembler, every endpoint agent that pattern-matches on decoded instructions, and every static analyser rests on assumptions about how bytes map to operations. Wherever those assumptions diverge from the hardware, you get evasion primitives that are invisible in your telemetry because your telemetry decoded the bytes differently than the CPU did. That is not hypothetical; it is the mechanism behind a long lineage of anti-analysis tricks. The remediation is unglamorous — healthy scepticism about single-source detection, and a second opinion for anything that matters.

Model Evaluation

DeepSeek V4 Flash 0731

ARC Prize publishes independent evaluation results for DeepSeek's latest fast model, adding another data point to a now-familiar pattern: open-weight releases arriving within months of frontier capability, at a small fraction of the inference cost.

Third-party benchmark results matter considerably more than launch posts, and ARC-style evaluations are useful precisely because they resist the memorisation that makes most leaderboards decorative. Whatever the specific scores, the quantity worth watching is the gap: how far behind the closed frontier a cheap, fast, openly available model now sits. That gap has been narrowing steadily, and each release compresses it further while dropping the price per token again. For anyone building workflows rather than models, this is the number that determines what becomes economically sensible to automate this quarter.

The security consequence of cheap, capable open weights is straightforward and rarely stated plainly: capability you can run locally is capability you cannot govern with an API key. Data-loss prevention built around blocking a handful of vendor domains quietly stops working the moment a competent model runs on a laptop or an unmanaged VM in someone's cloud account. That is not an argument against open weights, which have obvious research, cost and sovereignty benefits. It is an argument for governing data and endpoints rather than destinations — harder to do, and much harder to put on a slide.

Supply Chain Governance

The Nixpkgs core team has disbanded

The team responsible for stewarding Nixpkgs — one of the largest package collections in open source — has announced its own dissolution, leaving the governance of a widely depended-upon software supply chain in an openly unsettled state.

Package repositories fail slowly, and then suddenly. Nixpkgs is a genuinely enormous collection with a correspondingly enormous maintenance burden, and the disbanding of its core team is a governance event rather than a technical one: nothing breaks this morning. What changes is the answer to the questions defenders actually care about — who reviews privileged changes, who holds release and signing authority, how quickly a malicious commit would be noticed, and who is accountable when it is not. Maintainer strain in the Nix community has been visible for some time; this is the formal acknowledgement of it.

The xz backdoor established the template: patient social engineering of an under-resourced maintainer beats attacking a hardened build system every time. Every governance vacuum in a major ecosystem is an open invitation to run that play again, and the window is widest exactly when nobody is sure who is meant to be watching. If your organisation uses Nix anywhere — and in platform-engineering and AI shops it has quietly become common — this belongs on a watch list with a named owner rather than in somebody's memory. The question is not whether Nix is trustworthy today, but whether you would notice if it stopped being.

Managing AI Coding Costs at Scale

Databricks documents how it controls spend on AI coding assistants across a large engineering organisation — the attribution, quotas and usage visibility that become necessary once agentic tooling stops being an individual experiment and becomes a line item somebody has to defend.

This is the unglamorous half of agentic engineering, and the half that decides whether a programme survives its second budget cycle. The pattern will be familiar to anyone who has run cloud cost management: without per-team attribution, spend stays invisible until it is alarming; without visibility into which workflows consume tokens, optimisation is guesswork; and blunt caps simply push people onto personal accounts, which is a worse outcome than the cost. The useful move in the account is treating agentic usage as an operational workload with named owners and budgets, rather than as a perk or a pilot.

For anyone running AI-assisted development at a smaller scale, the same structure applies with far less machinery. Know which repositories and workflows generate the spend, know which of them produce merged work rather than abandoned branches, and be able to say what you would cut first if the bill doubled. The security overlap is direct and worth stating: the controls that give you cost attribution — organisation-managed accounts, org-level API keys, logged usage — are the same controls that stop source code and credentials leaving through personal subscriptions nobody can audit.

What to do

Move any AI coding tooling still running on personal accounts onto organisation-managed access this month; the cost visibility is a bonus, the audit trail is the actual point. Then instrument one number you can report without a spreadsheet exercise — spend per merged pull request, or per team per month — so the next budget conversation is evidential rather than anecdotal. If you run Claude Code or similar agentic tooling on your own workflows, set the same attribution up there first, before recommending it to anybody else.

Code Provenance
Directly applies to you

Oracle bans AI-generated code from OpenJDK

Oracle has barred AI-generated contributions from OpenJDK — a restriction that sits awkwardly beside its own executives' enthusiasm for AI-written software, and one that pushes every downstream consumer of the JDK to think about provenance rather than quality.

The underlying concern is almost certainly copyright and provenance rather than code quality. A project of OpenJDK's legal significance cannot comfortably accept contributions whose training-data lineage is unclear, because the exposure is not a bug but an ownership dispute over the runtime a very large share of enterprise software sits on. That is a defensible position. It is also unenforceable in any strong sense: no reviewer can reliably identify model-generated code from a diff, so in practice the policy operates as an attestation requirement placed on contributors rather than as a technical control.

The contradiction with executive messaging is the part worth noticing, because most organisations are living the same one. Leadership mandates AI-assisted development for velocity while legal and security functions have no settled answer on provenance, licence contamination, or accountability when generated code carries a vulnerability. OpenJDK has at least written its position down. Very few enterprises have, which means the de facto policy is whatever individual developers decided, undocumented, and impossible to evidence to a customer or an auditor asking an entirely reasonable question about how your software was produced.

What to do

Write down your organisation's position on AI-generated code this quarter, even if that position is permissive — an explicit stance beats an accidental one when someone asks for evidence. Cover attestation in the contribution or pull-request process, licence and provenance expectations for contractor and third-party code, and who signs off on generated code in regulated or safety-relevant components. Then ask your own suppliers what their policy is, because from today their answer is part of your supply chain whether you have read it or not.

The Human Cost

What happens if an entire class of workers loses faith in their careers

A long essay on the mood inside technology work — the serial layoffs, the automation anxiety, the sense that a career ladder has been quietly removed — and what it means when a profession that defined itself by optimism stops believing the story it told about itself.

The piece is more diagnosis than prescription, and it is better for that. Two decades of technology work were underwritten by an implicit bargain: intense hours in exchange for security, mobility and a plausible path upward. Serial layoffs, flattened middle rungs, and the arrival of tooling that automates precisely the junior work through which people used to learn their craft have all made that bargain look retrospective. The resulting mood is not simple burnout, which at least implies the possibility of recovery, but something closer to quiet disinvestment from the whole arrangement.

Security leaders should care about this for reasons beyond sympathy. Disengagement is the mechanism behind a large share of insider risk — not malice, but diminished care: shortcuts taken, alerts left unread, handovers never written. Institutional knowledge walks out with people who no longer see a reason to stay, and it was never in your documentation. Meanwhile the pipeline problem compounds: if entry-level analysis is the work agents now do, the profession needs a new answer for how anybody becomes senior. Nobody has produced one yet, and the clock is running.
